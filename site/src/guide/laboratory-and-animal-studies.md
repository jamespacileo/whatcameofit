## In the records

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) names two types of preclinical research: in vitro and in vivo. The National Cancer Institute's dictionary defines in vitro as in the laboratory, outside the body, and in vivo as in the body. Before a drug is tested in people, the FDA says, researchers must find out whether it has the potential to cause serious harm.

In the United States a sponsor must submit an Investigational New Drug application before clinical research begins. It includes the animal study and toxicity data, and the sponsor then waits 30 days while the FDA reviews it.

Trial registries record studies in people. This earlier work is found in published papers, not in registry records.

## What it does not tell you

A result in cells or in animals does not say what will happen in people. The FDA says preclinical research answers basic questions about safety and is not a substitute for studies of how a drug interacts with the human body.

It does not say that a trial in people will follow, or when.

“In vivo” does not by itself mean “in animals”. The term means in a living body.
